Arathon Challenge 03 · Full analysis

Anatomy of a Rural Uplink

7,144 UE samples around one Iowa base station, taken apart. The network is not coverage-limited in the way its downlink map suggests, the question has no single answer until you fix the service definition, and what actually fails is availability rather than speed.

01 — The dataset

What is actually in the file

Two collection days, four drive runs, one serving site. Agronomy Farm carries 3,838 samples; Curtiss (177) and Wilson Hall (106) appear sporadically and fit with R² ≈ 0, and Research Park never serves at all. This is a single-site study, and everything below treats it as one.

The 42% that most pipelines delete

3,023 rows report no serving cell — either a null cellid or the sentinel FFFFFFFFF with arfcn −1. A reflex dropna() removes them, and with them every coverage hole in the survey. They are not missing measurements. They are measured absences of service, and they are the demand the challenge asks us to serve.

03 — The model

Why physics, and not a gradient booster

A model trained on (lat, lon) → uplink can interpolate along a road it has seen. It cannot answer what if we put a transmitter over there, because it has no mechanism — it never saw a transmitter there. So the counterfactual runs through a fitted propagation law instead, where a node radiating X dB below the macro simply sits X dB lower on the curve at every distance.

Why a downlink measurement can predict uplink

ARFCN 630720 is band n78, which is TDD: uplink and downlink share one frequency, so path loss is reciprocal. That is what licenses the whole chain. On an FDD band this step would be invalid and there would be no counterfactual at all.

04 — Validation

The number a naive pipeline reports is fiction

Samples are 2.63 s apart — metres apart at driving speed. A random train/test split therefore tests the model on places it already trained on. We hold out whole KMeans blocks and drop every training row within 200 m of a test row.

05 — The decision

Where the asset goes, and how much it buys

931 demand cells at 200 m, weighted by passes rather than samples — a contiguous visit counts once, so the van parked beside the tower for 24 minutes does not outweigh 543 cells it drove through. 622 candidate sites, thinned from the driven route at 250 m spacing because rural siting follows roads, right-of-way and existing poles.

The finding that was not designed in

A donor-fed relay has to stay close enough to still hear the tower, so the feasibility filter drags it inward, to 4.2 km — where service is already mostly adequate. A backhauled small cell has no such leash and goes out to 7.1 km, where the need actually is. The cheap asset lands in the wrong place. This fell out of a donor-RSRP constraint; nobody asserted it.

06b — Which statistic?

The answer depends on whether you measure the average or the bad days

Everything above scores a cell on expected uplink. That is a defensible planning number, but it is not what a user experiences, and two things were hiding inside it. IsotonicRegression minimises squared error, so the fitted curve is a conditional mean — and throughput is right-skewed. More importantly, service is normally specified as a percentile: "10 Mbps at the 90th percentile of attempts", not "10 Mbps on average". Once outage is in the mixture those are wildly different tests.

The finding that reframes the whole problem

A cell that is unavailable more than 10% of the time has a p10 throughput of exactly zero, however fast it is when connected. 877 of 931 cells — 90.3% of route passes — are in that state. Under a reliability-based service definition, throughput is irrelevant for nine tenths of this corridor. It is an availability problem wearing a throughput problem's clothes, and no relay sized like ours changes that: one adds 0.72 points to a 9.1% baseline.

What we would now put in the recommendation

State the criterion before stating the site. On an average-throughput target the answer is a relay at 4.2 km, worth +1.67 points. On a 90%-reliability target there is no useful relay answer at all — the corridor needs macro-level coverage, because availability, not throughput, is what fails. Presenting the first number without the second would be the most misleading thing in this project.

08 — What the finding changed

Three things the percentile result forced us to rebuild

A number that swings tenfold on a choice nobody wrote down is not a result, it is a hidden assumption. Finding that the coverage figure and the recommended site both moved with the choice of statistic meant the system was answering a question it had never been asked to state. Three changes followed.

1service.py

The service definition became a parameter

It had been buried inside a helper as (1 − P(outage)) × mean uplink. There is now one module that owns "what does a cell deliver?", exposing mean, p05, p10, p50, p90 and availability behind a common interface, and config.py names the headline choice explicitly.

The headline default moved from mean to p50. IsotonicRegression minimises squared error, so the fitted curve is a conditional mean, and on a right-skewed throughput distribution reporting it flatters the network. p10 was not chosen either — with 90% of passes outage-limited it is nearly degenerate as an objective. p50 is the honest middle, and every criterion is now solved on every run regardless.

Effect — headline coverage restated from 51.4% to 29.6%, and its reported gain fell from 1.67 to 1.59 points, with the site itself moving 530 m.
2optimize.py

Availability became its own objective

If nine tenths of route passes are unavailable more than 10% of the time, optimising their throughput is optimising the wrong quantity — their reliable rate is zero whatever the radio does when it is up. So availability is now a first-class criterion with its own target, and the continuous version is reported in the unit a planner can act on: vehicle-passes restored to having service at all, rather than cells crossing a line.

The answer it gives is sobering, which is the point of measuring it. Optimised for the thresholded availability criterion the relay lands 3.7 km out; optimised for passes restored it lands 6.0 km out — two more distinct sites. And the best of them restores service to 10.6 of 1,380 passes, 0.8%.

Effect — the honest headline stops being "where to put a relay" and becomes "a relay of this class is not the intervention".
3consensus

A site that survives every definition — and the finding that none does

We do not get to know which statistic an operator will write into their requirement. So each criterion's gains are normalised by its own best, giving a regret in [0, 1], and the recommended site is the one whose worst regret across all definitions is smallest — minimax regret, the standard response to an unknown objective.

It helps, and it is not enough. The consensus relay reaches a worst-case regret of 0.77, against 0.85–1.00 for the single-criterion picks. Better, but 0.77 means that under its least favourable definition even the consensus site captures under a quarter of the achievable gain.

Effect — the deliverable stopped being a pin. The criteria place the relay at 4 distinct sites spanning 4.1 km (6 sites and 8.4 km for a small cell), so the operational output is now: fix the criterion first, because until you do the question does not have an answer.
What we would hand an operator now

Not a pin. A short question — is your requirement an average, a typical session, or a reliability guarantee? — and then one of three different answers, each with its own site, its own honest baseline, and in the reliability case the finding that no relay of this class is worth deploying, because availability rather than throughput is what fails and one asset restores 0.8% of it.

Also changed, while we were in there

  • The planner exposes the criterion. Switching between mean, p50, p10 and availability re-solves everything in the browser and moves the recommended pin, so the disagreement is something judges operate rather than read about. The consensus site is pinned alongside.
  • A palette collision was caught by the validator, not by eye: the status red sat at OKLab ΔE 13.8 from the ochre series, below the 15 floor for normal vision. Both were in the map at once. Replaced, and the check now runs over both themes on every build.
  • Duplication removed. The percentile machinery written for the one-off analysis became service.py, and the exploratory script now imports it rather than carrying its own copy of the lookup-table code.
07 — Conclusions

What the analysis supports, and what it does not

Supported

  1. This link is uplink-limited. 83% loss against downlink's 20%; 34.8% of uplink tests under 25 Mbps against 2.8% of downlink. The deficit is in the direction rural agriculture actually uses.
  2. The outage is real geography, not logging error. Spatially coherent, repeatable across days, monotone in distance.
  3. A physical model outperforms ML here on unseen geography (19.9 vs 21.0 MAE) while also being the only one that can evaluate an intervention.
  4. Optimised placement beats the worst-measured-point rule under every definition tested — 1.8× for a relay and 2.2× for a small cell on the p50 headline. The brief's hypothesis holds.
  5. The donor constraint mis-sites cheap assets, pulling relays into areas that are already served.
  6. The corridor is availability-limited, not throughput-limited. 90.3% of route passes are unavailable more than 10% of the time, which pins their reliable throughput to zero regardless of speed. Outage time is dominated by sustained episodes, not blips.

Not supported — and worth saying out loud

  • That one asset fixes this. It does not. One closes 3.4% of the deficit, five close 13%. The corridor needs a programme, not a box.
  • That we know the right pole. We know the right 2 km. The exact site is within the noise, and reporting 4% honestly is worth more than a confident wrong pin.
  • Any claim about congestion, interference, backhaul or scheduling. UE-side data cannot separate them. Every conclusion here is coverage- and power-limited only.
  • That these numbers generalise. One UE, one modem, one band, two days, four runs, one serving site.
  • That any single coverage figure is a fact about the network. It is a fact about the network and a choice of statistic. The same corridor is 94.8% or 9.1% covered under equally reasonable definitions, and the asset lands in 4 different places across them.
  • That this is a siting problem at all. Under a reliability requirement it is a coverage-build problem. The most useful thing the optimiser did was establish that its own answer is not worth deploying.
A seasonal caveat that cuts one way

Collection ran 19–20 March. Iowa row crops go in from late April and the deciduous tree lines were still bare, so this is a best-case foliage condition. The measured uplink deficit is a floor. Under full canopy at 3.46 GHz it gets worse, and the recommended site is therefore conservative.
